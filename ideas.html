Hard truths

Nine things I'd bet a sales engine on.

01

Focus beats volume.

The right customer, at the right time, beats ten times the activity. I disagree with "always be closing": a few of the right customers bring more revenue, sooner and for longer, than lots of small ones. Tell the rest, "we're probably not right for you."

02

Value at every step. Hold the price.

Every stage should give the buyer something: the first call, the demo, the proposal and the negotiation. When someone pushes on price, push back with value, not a discount. That takes confidence: you set the price for a reason, so hold it. Treat the product as valuable and buyers respect it more.

03

Fix upstream first.

Each layer caps the one below. No process sells to the wrong customer, and AI on a broken process only scales the break.

04

Most engines fight themselves.

SDRs book what AEs reject when nobody has defined a good deal. Write one definition, and use it everywhere.

05

The comp plan is the truth test.

It shows what a company really values, whatever the strategy deck says. People do what they're paid to do.

06

Every hour on a deal that's moving.

Return on effort. Busy teams push uphill on deals that won't close, and on admin no buyer ever sees.

07

Check the system before the person.

Do they know what's expected, can they do it, do they want to, does the system let them? Only one of those is about will.

08

The sale is the middle, not the end.

For a growing company, most revenue comes after the signature: first impact, renewal, expansion and referral.

09

Process can scale. Talent can't.

Build the way you sell into the CRM and the playbook, so the whole team and its agents can follow it, not just your best rep.

Two more I live by: trust is reliability, not niceness, and keep asking why until the buyer says, "that's exactly it."

Where AI belongs

Automate the legwork. Keep the moments that matter human.

The moments that matter are where a buyer decides whether to trust you. The work between them is work an agent can do.

  1. Research and enrichment
  2. The qualifying call
  3. Routing and follow-ups
  4. The demo
  5. Notes and CRM updates
  6. The stakeholders
  7. First drafts, checked
  8. The negotiation
  9. Renewal reminders
Agents do the legworkPeople own the moments that matter

Someone has to put their name to it, and the buyer has to feel it. People buy from people they like. AI can do a great deal, but on its own it feels cold.

My method

The 95% rule.

I don't scale an AI workflow until it sends what we would send, at least 95% of the time. One bad email to your best prospect can cost you the deal.

95%: let go
  1. 01

    Start with history

    Take your last 100 leads, and the replies your best people actually sent.

  2. 02

    Run it

    Let the agent research, score and draft a reply for every one.

  3. 03

    Score it

    Your best seller marks each draft: would I send this? If not, why not?

  4. 04

    Feed it back

    Turn the feedback into rules, examples and categories. Run the next 100.

  5. 05

    Check before send

    Go live with a person approving every message, and track how often they change it.

  6. 06

    Let go

    When the reviewer is just pressing send, send, send, automate it, and move to spot checks.

Where I broke my own rule. At UXCam we built an outbound idea I still like: show prospects what their own App Store reviews say, and offer to find out why they're losing customers. Then we scaled it too fast. We reached too many people, some in the wrong roles, with too few follow-ups from a team new to outbound. The lesson: smaller tests, faster, and get the message right before you add volume.
Working view · October 2026

What's changing in go-to-market.

What I'm seeing from inside sales teams right now. I'll update it as things change.

The mistake

Too much AI, too soon.

Teams replace too much of the human experience, scale before the message is right, and flood buyers with slop. Bad in, bad out.

Start here

Fix the leaky bucket first.

Find where you lose customers and time, then automate the repetitive work around it. Pour AI into a leaky bucket and you just lose more, faster.

Build

Design the system. Own the context.

A dozen point tools make a dozen silos. Build one system you control, with one source of truth, and grow it over time.

My prediction

The SDR becomes an agent operator.

Voice agents will ask the qualifying questions at a volume no team can match. A lead SDR runs them and joins the calls that go well.

The team

Fewer people, higher skill.

A strategic head of sales, a few skilled SDRs and AEs each running many agents, and RevOps keeping the system sharp.

Constant

The basics don't change.

The right customer, the right message at the right time, and a buyer who feels understood. AI makes each step faster. It skips none of them.
